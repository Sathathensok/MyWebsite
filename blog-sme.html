Why Cambodian SMEs Are Leaving Money on the Table

Three operational problems I keep seeing when consulting with small businesses in Phnom Penh — and practical fixes that don't require a large budget.

Over the past few years, alongside my full-time work in logistics, I've had the chance to consult with a range of small and medium-sized businesses in Phnom Penh. Trading companies, retail operations, service providers, and importers. Cambodian-owned, foreign-owned, and joint ventures.

Despite operating in different industries, the same three problems come up again and again. Not because the owners aren't capable — most are sharp, hardworking people who built something from nothing. But because certain operational blind spots are endemic to the SME stage of growth, and Cambodia's business environment amplifies them.

This article is not a lecture. It's a practical look at what I observe, why it matters, and what can realistically be done about it without a large capital investment.

Problem 1: Processes Live Inside People's Heads

In most Cambodian SMEs I've consulted with, the operating knowledge of the business sits primarily with two or three key people — often the owner and one or two long-tenured staff. When those people are absent, sick, or leave, the business slows significantly or stops functioning in critical areas.

This creates several compounding problems:

  • New staff take much longer to become effective, because onboarding is informal and inconsistent
  • Quality varies based on who does the task on a given day
  • The business cannot scale without the owner being personally involved in operations
  • When mistakes happen, it's difficult to identify where in the process they occurred

"A business that can only operate when the owner is watching has not yet become a real business."

The fix: Basic process documentation

You don't need expensive software or a consultant to document your core processes. You need someone to sit with the people who do the work and write down what they actually do, step by step.

Start with the five to ten processes that would cause the biggest problems if done inconsistently. Write them as simple checklists or flowcharts. Test them by having a new team member follow the document without guidance. If they can't, the documentation isn't complete enough.

This takes time upfront. But it's time you're already spending — you're just spending it answering the same questions repeatedly rather than building something that compounds over time.

Problem 2: Reporting is Backward-Looking and Late

Most SMEs I encounter produce some form of financial or operational reporting. The problem is typically one of timing and utility. Reports are often compiled manually at the end of the month, covering what happened three to four weeks ago, in a format that tells you whether things were good or bad — but not where to focus.

By the time a monthly report reaches a decision-maker, the window to course-correct on most operational problems has passed. You're reading a history book, not a dashboard.

The fix: Define three to five lead indicators and track them weekly

Rather than overhauling your entire reporting system, identify the three to five numbers that — if tracked weekly — would give you early warning of problems and a real-time sense of business health. These are different for every business, but common examples include: weekly cash collected vs. invoiced, customer complaints per week, order fulfilment time, and staff utilisation rate.

These don't need to be tracked in sophisticated software. A well-structured Excel file, updated consistently, will outperform an expensive system that nobody maintains. The discipline matters more than the tool.

Problem 3: Growth is Pursued Before Operations Are Stable

Cambodia's economy is growing, and with it, a real appetite for business expansion. New branches. New product lines. New markets. This ambition is healthy and worth supporting — but I regularly see businesses pursuing growth before the core operation is running with any consistency.

The result: the problems that exist at one location or in one product line get replicated and amplified across two or three. Staff are pulled thin. Customer experience suffers. The owner works longer hours managing chaos rather than building the business.

A useful test

Before expanding, ask honestly: if I stepped away for two weeks, would this business run acceptably well? If the answer is no, that's not a readiness problem — that's a sign that growth will make things harder, not easier.

The fix: Stabilise before you scale

This is simpler to say than to do, especially when opportunities appear. But the businesses that grow sustainably in Cambodia — and I've observed enough of them over the years — are the ones that resist the temptation to expand before their core operation is consistent, documented, and capable of running with reduced owner involvement.

A useful heuristic: if you cannot clearly explain to a new manager what "good" looks like in your operation, you're not ready to replicate it.

A Final Note on Budget

Everything I've described above can be done at low cost. Process documentation requires time, not money. Better reporting requires discipline, not expensive software. Operational stability requires focus, not large investment.

The most expensive operational problems I've seen in Cambodian SMEs were not caused by lack of resources. They were caused by lack of structure — and structure is something any business can build regardless of its size or stage.
