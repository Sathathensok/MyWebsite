How I Cut 70% of Manual Work at a Logistics Company

A behind-the-scenes account of deploying UiPath RPA in a real Southeast Asian enterprise — the wins, the friction, and what nobody tells you about automation projects.

In 2022, I was given a straightforward mandate at a top 1 global freight forwarding company: find ways to work smarter across our Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar operations. What followed was a two-year journey into process automation that resulted in measurable savings, a few bruised egos (mine included), and lessons that no certification course will prepare you for.

This is a practical account of what we actually did, what worked, and what I would do differently if I started over today.

Where We Started: The Honest Picture

Before any automation, our sea logistics teams were spending significant time on tasks that were repetitive, rule-based, and error-prone by nature: extracting data from booking confirmations, cross-referencing it against our internal system (SeaLog), updating tracking records, and generating status reports for clients.

None of these tasks required creative thinking. All of them required careful, consistent execution. That's precisely the profile that makes a process a good automation candidate — and it's also why human beings are poorly suited to it. We get tired, distracted, and bored. Systems don't.

"The best automation targets are the tasks that exhaust good people — not the ones that challenge them."

Choosing the Right Tool

We used UiPath as our primary RPA platform. It wasn't necessarily the cheapest option, but it had strong enterprise support, a solid exception-handling framework, and a large community — important when you're building something you need to maintain over years.

For lighter-weight, cloud-based workflows connecting SharePoint, Outlook, and Teams, we used Power Automate. The rule of thumb: UiPath for complex, multi-step desktop automation; Power Automate for anything already living in the Microsoft ecosystem.

We also used VBA for Excel-specific tasks. Some people dismiss VBA as outdated, but inside logistics operations where Excel is still the heartbeat of reporting, a well-built macro can save hours per week with zero licensing cost.

Tool Selection Principle

Don't choose your automation tool because it's the most powerful. Choose the one that best fits the task complexity and your team's ability to maintain it long-term. A complex UiPath workflow that nobody understands will become a liability within six months.

How We Selected What to Automate First

We mapped every repetitive task using a simple two-axis assessment: time consumed per week and rule-based consistency. Tasks that scored high on both — meaning they took significant time and followed a clear, consistent set of rules — went to the top of the list.

The first bots we deployed handled:

  • Extracting booking and shipment data from email attachments and mapping it into SeaLog
  • Generating daily status update reports and distributing them to the relevant teams
  • Auto-invoicing based on pre-agreed rate cards — this alone achieved an 86% adoption rate in Cambodia within months of launch
  • Monitoring key KPIs and flagging exceptions that required human attention

We deliberately left complex, judgment-heavy tasks for humans. Anything involving client negotiations, exception-handling decisions, or ambiguous documentation stayed off the automation roadmap entirely.

The Implementation: What Nobody Tells You

Here is what the UiPath certification courses do not adequately cover:

Change management is 60% of the work

When you tell experienced operations staff that a bot will handle part of their job, the reaction is rarely excitement. It's natural to feel unsettled. Some people worried about job security. Others were protective of the processes they'd built and refined over years.

We handled this by framing automation not as replacement but as reallocation. The time freed by automation was redirected toward client-facing work, quality checks, and problem-solving — the parts of the job that bots genuinely cannot do. This framing helped, but it required consistent reinforcement from management, not just a one-time announcement.

Bots break. Build for it from day one.

Any automation that touches an external system — an email inbox, a web portal, a legacy application — will encounter unexpected input at some point. A column header shifts. A PDF format changes. A portal update moves a button two pixels to the right.

We used UiPath's RE Framework (Robotic Enterprise Framework) from the start, which gave us structured exception handling, logging, and retry logic. Every bot had a designated human "exception owner" — someone responsible for reviewing failed transactions rather than assuming they didn't happen.

Log everything. Seriously.

We built a PostgreSQL logging system that captured every bot transaction: what was processed, when, what the outcome was, and how long it took. This served two purposes. First, it gave leadership visibility into actual productivity gains with hard data. Second, it helped us identify optimization opportunities — processes where bots were spending disproportionate time on low-value steps.

Technical Stack

UiPath Studio + Orchestrator · RE Framework · VB.NET for custom activities · PostgreSQL for transaction logging · Power BI for performance dashboards · Power Automate for Microsoft 365 integrations

Results: Honest and Measured

After 18 months of progressive deployment across three countries, the results were meaningful but not magical:

  • Manual processing time for routine shipment data tasks reduced by approximately 70%
  • Equivalent to a productivity boost of 12+ FTEs — staff who were reallocated to higher-value work
  • Monthly operational cost savings of approximately $10K+ across Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar
  • Cambodia's sea logistics KPI performance moved into the global top 10 — automation was one factor among several
  • Auto-invoicing adoption reached 86% within the Cambodia target market

I want to be honest about that last point: automation was a contributor to KPI improvements, not the sole cause. Process redesign, better training, and management alignment all played equal or greater roles. Anyone who attributes a KPI improvement entirely to their automation project is probably not accounting for the full picture.

"A bot running a bad process just fails faster. Fix the process first, then automate it."

What I'd Do Differently

If I were starting this programme from scratch today, I would:

  1. Spend twice as long on process documentation before touching UiPath. Every hour spent mapping and cleaning up a process before automation saves three hours of bot maintenance afterward.
  2. Pilot in one location before rolling out regionally. We moved faster than we should have in year one and paid for it with inconsistent results across countries.
  3. Assign dedicated bot maintenance ownership from day one. Bots are not set-and-forget. Without clear ownership, maintenance becomes nobody's job until something breaks in production.
  4. Build user dashboards earlier. Staff are more accepting of automation when they can see what it's doing in real time. Transparency reduces fear.

Automation, done well, is not about replacing people. It's about giving them back time to do the work that actually requires a human — judgment, relationships, creativity, and the ability to handle situations that no rule set can fully anticipate.
