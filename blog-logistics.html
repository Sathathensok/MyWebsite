Lessons from Running Regional Ops Across KH, TH & MM

What managing cross-border logistics across Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar teaches you about communication, local context, and knowing when to put down the system and pick up the phone.

Since April 2022, I've worked as the Thailand Cluster Sea Logistics and Process Order Management lead at Kuehne + Nagel, covering operations across Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar. Before that: DB Schenker handling sea freight key accounts, and DSV in air freight operations.

Somewhere between coordinating shipments across three very different regulatory environments and trying to get three very different teams aligned on a new system rollout, I collected a set of observations that I think are worth sharing — particularly for anyone stepping into a regional operations role in Southeast Asia for the first time.

These are not grand strategic frameworks. They're practical lessons from years of things going wrong and slowly figuring out why.

Each Country Is Its Own Operating Environment

This sounds obvious, but it's consistently underestimated — including by experienced logistics professionals. Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar share a region but operate with meaningfully different customs processes, documentation requirements, stakeholder relationships, and business cultures.

In Cambodia, the relationship between logistics providers and port authorities is a significant operational variable. Knowing the right contacts at Sihanoukville port and being known by them is not a soft advantage — it directly affects how quickly exceptions get resolved.

In Thailand, the system infrastructure is more developed and the processes more formalised, but the expectation of detailed, accurate documentation is correspondingly higher. Errors that might be resolved informally in Phnom Penh will be flagged formally in Bangkok.

Myanmar's environment is the most variable of the three — politically, logistically, and in terms of documentation expectations. Flexibility, local knowledge, and strong relationships with trusted partners on the ground are non-negotiable.

"A regional strategy that treats all three countries identically will fail in at least two of them."

Process Standardisation Has Limits

Part of my role has involved implementing regional process standardisation — aligning workflows, documentation, and system usage across three country operations. This is genuinely valuable. Consistency reduces errors, makes onboarding easier, and allows regional visibility into performance.

But I've also learned, sometimes the hard way, that pushing standardisation too far creates friction that exceeds its benefits. A process designed for Thailand's formal regulatory environment will generate unnecessary overhead if applied without adaptation to Cambodia. A system rollout that ignores the difference in local staff capability or infrastructure will struggle with adoption regardless of how well the system works elsewhere.

The balance I've landed on: standardise the core logic of processes (what needs to happen and in what order) while leaving room for local teams to adapt the execution to their environment. Write the "what" centrally; trust local leads to own the "how."

Training Is Not a One-Time Event

When we rolled out SeaLog in 2022 and subsequently implemented the SEA 2.0 strategic initiative across Cambodia and Myanmar, the training component was substantial. We delivered training sessions, produced documentation, ran Q&A workshops.

And then staff turned over. New processes were introduced on top of old ones. Edge cases appeared that training hadn't covered. Systems were updated without sufficient re-training.

The lesson: training is not an event that you complete. It's an ongoing responsibility, and the design of your processes should account for the fact that the team implementing them will be different six months from now than it is today.

What Changed How We Trained

We moved away from one-off training sessions toward maintaining updated SOPs that new team members could onboard from, combined with shorter, more frequent refresher sessions after any significant process or system change. Quality improved noticeably within two quarters.

Know When to Use the System and When to Use the Phone

This is the lesson I mention most often when talking to younger logistics professionals: systems are tools, not substitutes for human judgment and relationships.

Across all three countries, the moments that most affected customer outcomes — positively and negatively — were not determined by whether the system had the right data. They were determined by whether the right person made a call at the right time and had enough relationship capital with a counterpart to get something done outside of the standard process.

A customs issue that would take three days to resolve through formal channels can sometimes be resolved in three hours by a well-placed phone call from someone who is known and trusted. No system automates that. You build it over years, one interaction at a time.

Continuous Improvement Is a Practice, Not a Project

One of the ongoing responsibilities I've found most meaningful is delivering process improvement and operational excellence initiatives across the three countries — contributing to what K+N frames as corporate strategic goals.

The trap with this kind of work is treating it as a project with a completion date. You map a process, redesign it, implement the change, and move on — only to return six months later and find that the team has drifted back to the old way of doing things, or that the environment has changed enough that the redesigned process no longer fits.

Sustainable operational improvement requires a mindset shift within the team — not just a process change. People need to understand why the change exists, how it connects to the broader goal, and have enough ownership of the outcome to maintain it when the external attention moves on.

"Change the environment, not just the process. Processes revert. Environments shape behaviour consistently."

A Final Note

Managing regional operations across Southeast Asia is genuinely demanding — the complexity is real and the stakes are high when a supply chain disruption affects a major client's production line.

But it's also some of the most interesting work I've done. Every country is a different problem-solving context. Every client relationship is different. And the satisfaction of building something that actually works — a process that runs smoothly, a team that operates with clarity, a system that delivers what it promises — is real and lasting.
