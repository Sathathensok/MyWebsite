Lessons from Running Regional Ops Across KH, TH & MM

What managing logistics across Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar taught me about people, processes, and knowing when to put down the system and pick up the phone.

I've spent my career working in logistics at some of the world's leading freight forwarding companies — across sea freight, air freight, and regional cluster operations covering Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar.

Over those years, I've learned a lot from both the wins and the challenges. This post shares the lessons I keep coming back to — the kind that only make sense after you've been responsible for real outcomes across multiple countries at the same time.

I hope they're useful if you're working in — or moving into — a regional logistics role in Southeast Asia.

Every Country Works Differently

Cambodia, Thailand, and Myanmar are neighbours, but they operate very differently. Customs rules, paperwork requirements, and the way business gets done are not the same across borders.

In Cambodia, relationships matter a lot. Knowing the right people — and being known by them — helps you solve problems faster. That network takes time to build, but it's worth every effort.

In Thailand, things are more formal and structured. Processes are more developed, and getting your documentation right consistently pays off in a big way.

In Myanmar, flexibility is key. Local knowledge and trusted partners on the ground make the biggest difference. The skills you build here — adaptability, local awareness, strong relationships — are useful across the whole region.

“A tailored strategy for each of the three countries will allow the company to perform strongly in all of them.”

Standardisation Is a Tool, Not the Goal

Having consistent processes across countries is genuinely valuable. It reduces mistakes, makes training easier, and gives you a clear picture of how the whole operation is performing.

But there's a balance to strike. A process built for one country won't always work the same way in another. Pushing too hard for uniformity can create more friction than it solves.

What works best: agree on the core steps — what needs to happen and in what order — then let local teams decide how to execute those steps in their own environment. Set the "what" centrally. Trust your local leads on the "how."

Training Never Really Stops

Every big system rollout I've led included training — workshops, guides, Q&A sessions. That initial investment is important. But what matters just as much is what comes after.

People change. Processes evolve. Systems get updated. If your training stops at go-live, the gap between what people know and what they need to know will quietly grow over time.

The shift that made the biggest difference for me was moving from one-off training events to keeping SOPs updated and running short refreshers whenever something changed. A team that truly understands its tools works better, finds its own efficiencies, and needs fewer workarounds.

What Made the Difference

Replacing one-off training sessions with up-to-date SOPs and short, regular refreshers after process changes led to a clear improvement in team performance within two quarters. Treating documentation as a living tool — not a project deliverable — was the key shift.

Systems Help. Relationships Solve.

Here's the lesson I share most with younger logistics professionals: a system gives you information. It doesn't make decisions, and it can't build trust.

The moments that shaped outcomes most — for better or worse — were rarely about whether the data in the system was correct. They were about whether the right person made a call at the right time, with enough trust built up to get something done quickly.

A customs issue that takes three days to resolve through formal channels can sometimes be handled in three hours by someone who is known and trusted. No system automates that. You build it one interaction at a time, over years.

Improvement Is Ongoing, Not a One-Time Fix

Process improvement is some of the most rewarding work in regional operations. When it works, the results are real and lasting — cleaner workflows, stronger teams, better numbers.

The key is to treat it as an ongoing practice, not a one-time project. When people understand why a change is happening and feel ownership over it, they maintain it. When they don't, things gradually drift back to how they were before.

The goal isn't just to change the process. It's to change how the team thinks about the process.

"Change the environment, not just the process. Processes revert. Environments shape behaviour consistently."

A Final Note

Managing regional logistics across Southeast Asia is demanding work. The complexity is real, and so are the stakes when something goes wrong in a client's supply chain.

But it's also some of the most interesting work I've done. Every country is a new kind of challenge. Every client is different. And building something that actually works — a process that runs smoothly, a team that knows what it's doing, a system that delivers — is deeply satisfying.
